# Telecommunications in Rural PRL — 1960s

## Overview

Telephone access in rural Poland during the 1960s was virtually nonexistent. The Bieszczady region — already the most isolated and underdeveloped part of the country — was even worse off than the average village.

## Key Facts

- **One phone per village** was typical — located at the post office, gmina office, or sołtys's house. Not in private homes.
- **Manual switchboard operators** connected calls. You picked up, asked the operator for a number, and waited. Quality was terrible, connections dropped, bad weather could knock lines out entirely.
- **The operator (telefonistka)** worked a manual exchange (ręczna centrala), usually in the back of a post office. She physically joined callers by plugging cord pairs into the board. Because she bridged every conversation, **she could overhear any call** — privacy did not exist. In a small exchange of a few dozen numbers she often knew every subscriber by name.
- **Long-distance calls (międzymiastowe)** had to be routed through one or more intermediate town exchanges. You booked the call and waited — minutes to hours — for the connection ("proszę czekać na połączenie"). A remote valley's calls to the outside world therefore passed through a larger town exchange, and whoever sat at that board was the bottleneck for reaching anyone beyond the district.
- **Waiting lists** for a private phone were **10–15 years** in cities. On a Bieszczady village? Forget it.
- **Common models:** RWT CB-621, CB-662, AWS-1 — heavy bakelite handsets, rotary dial or crank-operated.
- **Priority access** went to state institutions, party officials, PGR state farms, and the military. A sołtys with a phone in his office was a man with real power — he controlled the village's only link to the outside world.

## Telegraph (Telegram)

The telegraph was not a relic by the late 1960s; it was in common everyday use, run by the state enterprise **Polska Poczta, Telegraf i Telefon (PPTiT)** (1950–1991). Its very name made telegraph one of the three pillars of communication.

- **How you sent one:** at the post-office window. You paid **by the word**, so messages were clipped and formulaic, e.g. "ARRIVING THURSDAY STOP."
- **How it arrived:** delivered as a written slip by a listonosz or a dedicated messenger (doręczyciel), often the same day.
- **Why it mattered:** home phones were rare (a private line meant a 10–15 year wait), so the telegram was the standard way to send anything urgent: deaths, births, an arrival, wedding and name-day wishes.
- **The technology:** by the 1960s the classic Morse key was largely replaced by teleprinters (dalekopisy) that sent text automatically; the teleks network between institutions and firms was growing.
- **Relay by phone:** in small offices an incoming telegram was often taken down over the telephone line between exchanges and transcribed by the operator or postal clerk, then handed to a messenger for delivery. Telegrams outlasted the century, fading only as phones, mobiles, and the internet spread.
- **In our depopulated Bieszczady village:** post, telephone, and telegraph are the same tiny PPTiT office and, plausibly, the same person. The operator sits directly in the relay path: she receives, transcribes, and releases the slip. A telegram also leaves a paper trail and names a sending office, so a telegram "from" a man twelve years dead is impossible in a traceable way, which is exactly what makes a faked one a deliberate tell.

## What This Means for the Story

- The committee cannot easily contact the outside. The only phone is in Zbigniew Gajda's office — and he decides who uses it.
- If the road is mud and the phone is controlled, the committee is **cut off**. No backup, no independent reporting.
- por. Witold Skowron needs Zbigniew Gajda's phone to reach his superiors — another thread binding them together.
- A broken phone line (storm, "accident") could isolate the village completely.
- A **faked telegram** is period-plausible and deniable: the operator is the one who takes it down and hands it over, and a dead man's telegram is an impossible object players can keep and puzzle over.
