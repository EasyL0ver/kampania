# Bieszczady Terrain — Ridges, Water-Gaps & Landslide Dams

Reference for the flood arc's physical mechanism: why %NEW_VILLAGE% floods, what the state's map got wrong, and what [Michał Pytlak](../characters/foreman.md) blows up to save it. All of it is grounded in the real geology of the Bieszczady.

## The Landscape — "Ladder" Ridges and Trellis Rivers

The Bieszczady are built of **Carpathian flysch** — alternating layers of hard **sandstone** and weak **shale** (łupki), folded and tilted. This produces a distinctive **"ladder" (rusztowa) structure**: long, parallel mountain ridges of resistant sandstone running roughly SE–NW, separated by long **valleys** cut in the softer shale between them.

The rivers follow a **trellis (kratowy) pattern**:
- Main streams run **along** the valleys, parallel to the ridges.
- Then they **cut transversely straight through a ridge**, forming a **water-gap** — in Polish a **przełom**. A river punching a narrow notch through a hard sandstone ridge to reach the next valley is the signature landform of the region.

**Passes (przełęcze) form where the shale is** — the low saddles that break a ridge sit on the weak shale bands, not the hard sandstone. The soft rock is where the ridge is lowest, thinnest, and most erodible.

## Landslides That Dam Valleys

Because hard sandstone sits on slip-prone shale, **landslides (osuwiska) are extremely common** in the Bieszczady — especially after heavy rain or snowmelt saturates the shale and a block of hillside lets go. These slides regularly **dam valleys**, plugging a watercourse with loose debris and impounding water behind it.

The famous real example:

- **Osuwisko Zwiezło (1907)** — on the western slopes of Chryszczata near Duszatyn. After intense rain and snowmelt, a huge section of slope slid into the valley of the Olchowaty stream. Roughly **12 million m³** of earth and broken rock moved along a 2 km front and **dammed the valley**, creating the **Jeziorka Duszatyńskie** — landslide-dam lakes still there today (now a nature reserve, Zwiezło).

Two things this establishes for the campaign, both real:
1. **Loose rock barriers block valleys here.** A slide plugging a gap and backing up water is ordinary Bieszczady behaviour, triggered by exactly the kind of heavy rain the flood arc runs on.
2. **Those barriers are debris, not bedrock.** A landslide plug is broken rock and earth — erodible and, crucially, **breachable.** Once a notch is cut in it, running water scours it wide on its own (documented breach-widening / headcut erosion in loose material). You do not have to move a mountain; you crack the plug and the flood finishes it.

## The Lake Finds the Lowest Way Out — Spillways

When a landslide dams a valley, the impounded water does not just sit still. It **rises as a lake** until it reaches the lowest point on the rim of the basin — a **próg, siodło, or przełęcz (a spillway / col)** — and **spills over** there. If that overflow point is a low saddle between ridges, the escaping water carves a fresh notch and, over time, cuts a new przełom (a *przełom przelewowy*, overflow gorge). The lake keeps rising only until it finds such an outlet.

This is the hydrological hinge of the flood arc:

- A sealed valley is not automatically doomed. The rising water will escape at **whatever outlet sits lowest** — and if that outlet is below the settlement, the water leaves before it ever reaches the houses.
- The village drowns **only if every possible spillway sits above house level or cannot pass the flow**, so the lake tops the village before it finds any way out.
- That turns "will it flood?" into a concrete field question: **find every candidate outlet and check whether any lies low enough (and wide enough) to relieve the backup before it reaches the village.** The primary outlet is the plugged przełom; the secondary candidates are a natural col (an old streambed spilling into the next valley) and any man-made cut (a PGR irrigation ditch). Rule them all out and the flood is proven.

## How This Drives the Flood Arc

The terrain feature at the centre of the flood arc: a **sandstone ridge separates the [old-village](../locations/old-village-ruins.md) valley (the lower basin, the natural sink) from the [%NEW_VILLAGE%](../locations/wojewodas-house.md) valley (on the river's flood path).** The ridge has a **natural water-gap (przełom)** through it at a low shale saddle — and that gap has been **plugged by an old landslide.**

- **Why the new village floods:** the plug seals the gap, so the swollen river can't drain through into the lower old-village basin where it belongs. It backs up and rises onto the new village instead.
- **Why the state's map is wrong:** the old military survey map shows the water-gap **open** — true when the map was drawn. The landslide that closed it came later and was never recorded. The state planned off a map that was correct once and quietly went obsolete: on paper, water drains to the low basin and the new village sits safe above it. On the ground, the plug puts the water on the houses. Honest negligence, not stupidity.
- **What the geologist finds:** field measurement reveals the plugged gap and the true drainage — the flood has nowhere to go but up onto the village.
- **What Pytlak blows:** the **landslide plug** in the gap — loose debris, not the hard ridge. One charge opens a breach; the flood scours it into a full water-gap again. Water pours from the new-village valley through the reopened przełom into the old-village basin. The new village drains; the old village drowns — which it does anyway, from this flood and from the reservoir that will bury it forever.
