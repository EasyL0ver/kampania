# Death Customs, Corpse Storage & Decomposition in Rural PRL

Covers how long a body could realistically remain unburied in a 1960s Bieszczady village, why, and what it would look and smell like. Grounds the flood-delayed funeral and the "body taken from the church" beat.

## Wake customs — how long a body stayed above ground

On the Polish village into the PRL era, the dead were kept **at home for 2–3 days** before burial, not in a funeral home — rural Poland had no mortuary refrigeration and no routine embalming.

- The body was washed and dressed by older neighbour women (not usually the closest family), eyes and mouth closed, hands folded on the chest.
- It was laid out in the **coldest room in the house** — on straw or a "grave board" (*deska*) over benches, under a sheet — deliberately, to slow decay.
- Neighbours and the whole village came to pray, sing funeral hymns, and keep vigil. The last night before burial was the **pusta noc** ("empty night") — an all-night prayer vigil.
- Custom held the body should be taken out **by the third day**. This was partly practical (decomposition) and partly belief: a corpse kept too long was thought to "pull" the next death in the family after it. A body still unburied past that window is a source of real communal unease.

**Campaign use:** A 2–3 day wake is normal. By day 5–6 the village is *already* uneasy that Janina has been kept too long — that unease is free atmosphere, and it is exactly the pressure the "someone had to deal with it" framing exploits.

## Decomposition in the cold — the timeline

Decomposition is driven by temperature. Below ~10°C the putrefactive (rotting) stage is strongly slowed; below ~4°C it is nearly arrested. An unheated stone/wood church in a cold, rain-soaked autumn (roughly 5–12°C) acts as a slow, natural cold store.

- **Rigor mortis:** sets 2–6 h after death, peaks ~12–24 h, normally resolves in 24–48 h — but **cold prolongs it to 3–4 days**.
- **Putrefaction (bloating, green marbling on the abdomen, odour):** in a warm room this begins ~day 2–3. In cold damp conditions it is **pushed back several days** and progresses slowly.
- **A body dead ~3–4 days in a cold church** would be cold, stiffening or just past stiff, discoloured, faintly and increasingly foul — grim, but recognisable and physically intact, not liquefying. A closed pine coffin further contains the odour.
- A barbiturate (Luminal) overdose death does not change any of this — no wounds, nothing to speed or slow decay.

**Bottom line:** 3–4 days unburied is comfortably realistic; even ~5 days holds in cold conditions. But the smell is real and growing — enough that a butcher could plausibly say the body "was starting to turn."

## Flood postpones the whole funeral

A serious flood doesn't just make one grave hard to dig — it can stop the funeral entirely:

- **No grave.** Waterlogged or submerged cemetery ground can't be dug; a dug grave fills with water.
- **No procession or gathering.** Washed-out roads cut off the *kondukt* (procession to the cemetery), out-of-parish mourners, and the assembly of the village.
- **Priest and community occupied** with the flood emergency.

So under a real flood, the honest outcome is not "burial on schedule" but **the funeral is postponed and the body waits** — in the church or the home — until the water drops. This is period- and terrain-plausible for the Bieszczady, and it is a consequence of the flood the story already establishes, not a contrivance.
